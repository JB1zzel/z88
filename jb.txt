'You just know crap when you see it': Meet Jamie
Bradbury, judge of the world's worst video game
contest

The 2023 Crap Games Contest invites programmers
to create the most terrible games possible for a
retro British computer — and one man has to play
them all

By Sheena Goodyear
CBC Radio · As It Happens
Posted: July 7, 2023

Jamie Bradbury has a job most people wouldn't
envy. He spends his days playing deliberately
terrible video games — and writing thoughtful
reviews of each one.

The 44-year-old from Hull, England, is the host
and sole judge of the 2023 Comp.Sys.Sinclair
Crap Games Contest, an annual tongue-in-cheek
competition where programmers compete to create
the worst possible games for the ZX Spectrum,
one of the UK's oldest home computers.

"There's no hard and fast criteria, really,"
Bradbury told As It Happens host Nil Köksal in an
interview for CBC Radio. "You just know crap when
you see it."

From lawnmowers to cocaine bears

The contest has humble origins. In 1988, the
computer magazine Your Sinclair published a
glowing review of a game called Advanced
Lawnmower Simulator — which didn't actually
exist — as an April Fools' joke. A month later,
they released an actual copy on a cassette tape
attached to the magazine's cover.

Readers began sending in their own terrible
games, and the magazine launched a column called
Crap Game Corner. The column eventually morphed
into the annual contest that continues today.

This year's entries include Cocaine Bear, loosely
based on the 2023 film (which was itself loosely
based on a true story); Face Punch, where the
player slowly moves back and forth punching an
opponent until he falls down; SUPER (advanced
fishing simulator), a fishing simulator that is,
in fact, not very advanced at all; and multiple
games based on the popular drawing toy Etch A
Sketch.

Bradbury's job is to play and review every single
submission. He's done nearly 30 so far and will
continue reviewing games as they roll in through
December 16.

"Imagine if you've got five different Etch A
Sketch games and you've got to write a review on
each one and they're all essentially more or less
the same thing," he said.

To the loser goes the crown

So how did Bradbury end up with such a tedious
responsibility? It's his reward — or punishment
— for being too good at the competition last
year.

Every year, the person whose game is judged to be
the "least crappy" is tasked with hosting and
judging the following year's competition.

Bradbury earned the position by creating Isle of
Jobo, a game where players control a corrupt
prime minister trying to hold onto power while
lining his own pockets and those of his cronies.

"I'm proud of the game I made, definitely,"
Bradbury said.

But he's got mixed feelings about his victory.

"There's definitely a lot of work that's involved
to run a year-long competition where you're
reviewing games with a very mixed quality
standard," he said. "It's quite a lot of pressure
to think of something novel and interesting to
say with a game that might have taken someone
five minutes to make, and it doesn't really do
anything."

The charm of retro gaming

All submissions are designed for the ZX Spectrum,
a system released in 1982 that could display only
eight basic colours and had 90,000 times less
memory than a modern iPhone. For Bradbury, that's
part of the appeal.

"People of my generation, it's the first computer
that people had in their homes. So I think
there's a lot of great childhood memories and
nostalgia connected to it," he said.

The games are all free to download, and modern
gamers can play them using an emulator — a
program that simulates the ZX Spectrum experience
on a computer.

Bradbury says the "crap" factor can come from
many places. Some entries are technically inept
— the creator had big ambitions but not enough
programming skill. Others lean heavily into a
particular sense of humour that doesn't quite
land.

"If you have great ambition, but maybe your skill
level as a programmer doesn't meet that ambition,
then that could make something crap," he said.
"If you've got a certain sense of humour and you
want to sort of build a game around that, then
that could be quite crap."

What's next

The contest ends Dec. 16, at which point Bradbury
will decide which game is the most crap, and
which is the least crap — thus passing the
hosting duties to another unfortunate soul for

2024. 

"I've always found it tremendously amusing to
sort of quickly code a crap game over lunch and
then see what people think about it," he said.
"It's nice to be part of that history, really."

And what about the power of being the sole
arbiter of crapness?

"I'll try not to let the power go to my head,"
he joked.
