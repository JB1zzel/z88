The Sinclair QL, A Quantum Leap to Where?

When we think of Sir Clive Sinclair, our minds often 
jump to the ubiquitous Spectrum, the pocket 
calculator, or, infamously, the C5 - An electric 
tricycle for a dream Britain that never was. And it's 
this last project that holds a key to understanding 
the fate of one of his most ambitious computers: the 
Sinclair QL (Quantum Leap).

By 1984, Sinclair's attention was famously divided. 
His passion project, the C5, was to be a revolutionary 
personal transport solution for the masses, rivalling 
the car. History, of course, judges it a commercial 
disaster. A good product that failed to find its 
market. In a twist of irony, had it been marketed as 
a toy rather than serious transport, it might have 
found the success it later enjoyed as a curiosity. But 
the C5's catastrophic failure consumed Sinclair's 
focus and capital at the very moment its technological 
sibling, the QL, was fighting to be born.

The QL was conceived as a professional machine, a step 
beyond the hobbyist Spectrum. It was a bold vision: a 
sleek, black "slab" of computing power with a built-in 
multi-tasking operating system (or pre-emptive 
multitasking as they called it). And it still looks 
stunning today. Sinclair understood that aesthetics 
sold technology, and he never made a bad looking 
product.

Yet, beneath its stylish exterior lay two critical, 
self-inflicted wounds.

First, the bespoke keyboard. Determined to avoid the 
"dead flesh" keys of the Spectrum, Sinclair 
commissioned a unique, custom solution. In typical 
Sinclair fashion, it defiantly broke convention. The 
key shapes were unusual, the layout was non-standard – 
there is no Delete or Backspace key, a startling 
omission for a machine aimed at business. The 
baffling truth? Standard, off the shelf keyboard parts 
would have provided a familiar, efficient typing 
experience and been cheaper to manufacture. Sinclair's 
insistence on a custom, idiosyncratic solution drove 
costs up and produced a worse typing experience.

But if you think that's an own goal, just wait.

Second, and more damaging, were the Microdrives. These 
innovative but fragile tape-loop storage systems were 
pushed to run at speeds beyond their reliable limits. 
This engineering hubris caused massive delays, as the 
team wrestled with chronic reliability issues. The 
lesson was clear: had Sinclair used existing, stable 
Microdrive technology and a conventional keyboard, the 
QL would have reached the market sooner, cheaper, and 
with far fewer teething troubles.

I'm not for one second suggesting my solutions would 
have fixed every gripe. No disk drive or mouse 
environment were key omissions out of step with a 
market rushing towards IBM compatibility. But my fixes 
would have brought a more reliable product to market 
quicker and cheaper. It's not every day you can make a 
change that results in cheaper, better and quicker – 
this is arguably one of those rare exceptions.

But ah, the gift of hindsight! The future was very 
different. The C5 bankrupted the company, forcing its 
sale to Alan Sugar, who canned the QL immediately.

Despite these profound flaws, the QL's legacy is 
surprisingly secure. It brought the first true 
multi-tasking operating system to a pre-packaged home 
computer. A genuine quantum leap in concept. And while 
commercial success eluded it, the machine forged a 
passionate, international community of devotees who 
continue to champion its unique character.

You might think the story ends in the mid-80s, but 
you'd be wrong. This small, hardcore following has 
nurtured a remarkable homebrew scene. In the last few 
years, developers have pushed the humble QL to places 
Sinclair could only have dreamed of, creating games 
that are not just nostalgic curiosities, but 
incredible technical achievements. Let's explore (In 
my own personal idiosyncratic order) some of the most 
notable titles that have brought this classic machine 
back to life…

Batman (2021-2025) Joan Gayón

If the Sinclair QL had enjoyed the commercial life it 
deserved, Jon Ritman and Bernie Drummond's iconic 
Batman (1986) would surely have been ported to the 
machine. It never was. The QL was discontinued by 
Amstrad just weeks before the game's release. But that 
omission has now been spectacularly corrected.

Spanish programmer Joan Gayón, driven by childhood 
nostalgia and technical curiosity, spent years 
mastering the QL's idiosyncratic hardware to craft not 
merely a port, but a definitive reimagining. His 
version transforms the original Spectrum's monochrome 
isometric world into a glorious, full colour 
experience, exploiting the QL's eight colour palette 
with astonishing artistry. Every one of the original 
screens is present, every puzzle intact, every enemy 
and bat-gadget rendered with fluid precision.

Written in pure 68000 assembler and requiring a 640k 
memory expansion, it is a technical tour de force that 
doesn't simply rival the original – it surpasses it 
with one bold quantum leap. More than four years in 
the making, Gayón's Batman is not just another 
homebrew title. It is an epic journey of retro 
devotion that stands as one of the finest games ever 
released for the Sinclair QL, and a powerful testament 
to the machine's enduring, international community.

Old Tower (2026) – Jim McKay

Old Tower continues where Batman left off. Dazzling, 
it's a lavish, colour rich reimagining of a modern 
Spectrum classic.

Originally developed by Russian studio RetroSouls for 
the ZX Spectrum in 2018, Old Tower was a competition 
entry inspired by the mobile hit Tomb of the Mask, 
notable for being the Spectrum's first ever 
multicolour scroller. Fast forward to 2026, and 
programmer Jim McKay has delivered a ground up, 
bespoke conversion for the Sinclair QL that is, for 
me, the definitive version.

Everything is here. All the levels are faithfully 
recreated, with splendid full colour graphics, buttery 
smooth vertical scrolling, and optional AY music via 
Qsound (many emulators support this hardware addon). 
McKay even offers two editions: a lean 57KB build for 
128K machines and a generously expanded version with 
five compressed music tracks for systems with 256K or 
more. It runs from a single Microdrive.

The controls are tight, the puzzles are challenging, 
and the presentation is polished to a professional 
standard. Old Tower is not merely a competent port; 
it's yet another masterclass in how to translate a 
modern retro game to the QL with love, technical 
skill, and deep respect for the source material.

Android 2 (2025) – Spkr (Netherlands) and Ggn (Greece)

For this QL conversion Spkr and Ggn came together to 
prove Spectrum games can shine when implemented on 
their favoured machine. Android 2, Vortex Software's 
1986 isometric snake-hunting cult classic, was too 
hard for me on the Spectrum. Their QL port runs at a 
saner speed, toning the challenge down just enough to 
make the game feel like a fun challenge rather than an 
impossible feat of reflexes and dexterity.

The QL version runs about 20% slower, and this is a 
very good thing. You have time to think. The game 
breathes. You get to act tactically, plan your moves, 
review the lay of the land, and then attack those evil 
snakes at a time and place of your choosing.

Everything else, from the intro sequence to the 
isometric charm, remains intact. The team made an 
artistic decision to keep true to the original 
graphics rather than enhance them – a wise decision, 
because the game looks great already. Android 2 on the 
QL was one I really had a blast playing. A truly 
impressive conversion.

Curse of Rabenstein (2025) – Alan Pemberton & Andrei 
Tenu

For Halloween, Pemberton and Tenu ported a game that 
has become something of a horror classic on the 
Spectrum Next: Curse of Rabenstein. Paul Jenkinson's 
2025 DAAD powered, vampire slaying adventure is great 
for beginners because of its short length, generosity 
with clues, and puzzles that rarely demand complex 
solutions.

The QL version arrived with two editions on one disk: 
a classic 8 colour build for standard machines, and a 
glorious SMSQ/E version supporting 256 colours. Alan 
and crew made it easy peasy for total QL noobs too. 
They have a version packaged with an emulator – just 
unzip and play. I was also able to play the 256 colour 
version from the disk image on QPC2, which looks 
incredibly slick.

This is probably the perfect place to jump in first. 
It's a great game, and it's a bridge – taking you from 
the familiar comfort of the ZX Spectrum to the real 
Spectrum Next, the QL, holding your hand all the way.

Space Invaders (2024) – Simon Greenaway

Sometimes the old ideas are the best, and sometimes 
the simplest ideas are the hardest. Simon Greenaway's 
Space Invaders is exactly what it says on the tin: "as 
accurate to the original arcade game as I can be 
bothered". And can I just say, he bothered. A lot.

The result is a port so smooth, so faithful, it feels 
less like a homebrew clone and more like the 
professional conversion the QL should have had in 
1984. Greenaway made a game that runs impressively on 
original hardware and absolutely flies on turbocharged 
QLs with Gold Cards (which is how I played it). Silky 
framerate, precise hit detection, that unmistakable 
descending dread.

This is the arcade original, rendered in the QL's 
eight colour palette, running on forty year old 
British computing's most brilliantly bonkers hardware. 
No gimmicks. No isometric puzzles. Just rows of 
aliens, a single laser cannon, and the relentless 
ticking clock of their descent. Perfect.

Pitman 2024

Catrap is a Japanese exclusive hidden gem on the Game 
Boy. Think of the crate pushing Sokoban games as a 
side on spectacle, complete with platforming staples 
of ladders and collapsible platforms. Andrew has 
produced a spectacular port of this game using the 
QL's SuperBASIC in the form of Pitman.

Andrew is no slouch when it comes to making impressive 
games and software for the QL. His similar titles 
include Qstar and Qstar2, both classic crate shifting 
games from the 80s. I chose Pitman to showcase today, 
but his other games deserve a look too.

It's so impressive that games of this quality can be 
produced in SuperBASIC. Pitman contains some quite 
challenging puzzles, but its gentle learning curve 
gives you a fighting chance. Well worth checking out – 
it's a truly great port.

Conclusion

Well, sadly, this brings me to the end of this 
feature. One I have really enjoyed exploring.

At the start, the QL was relatively unfamiliar to me, 
and I confess it's not always straightforward to 
navigate its vast (and I do mean vast) ecosystem. 
Aftermarket ROMs, software, hardware addons and – 
excitingly – new and more powerful machines like 
cloned Gold Cards, SD interfaces and the Q68 await the 
curious explorer. But it's in part this mysterious 
tinkerers' warren of revision and development branches 
that keeps the QL so exotic in the first place.

All in all I count 24 new games for the QL in the last 
three years. That's nowhere near as many as the humble 
Speccy, but that sort of thinking misses the point. 
Visit the forums (Spanish and English) for yourself, 
and find a vibrant scene of friendly folk up to all 
sorts of technical wizardry that doesn't sit neatly in 
a gaming article. And that's very fitting, because it 
shows the QL was more than the cheap gaming machine 
that was the Spectrum's niche.

In that way I think Sinclair gets the last laugh. On 
its launch, the magazines scoffed: "A Quantum Leap to 
where?" Forty years later the community answers as 
loudly as ever – to endless tinkering! And isn't that 
what a computer was meant to be? A bicycle for the 
mind.
